# December 2026 devotional library

Series: **Light in the Waiting**

Original devotional writing. Scripture references resolve from the selected Bible translation.

## 2026-12-01 — A Light in the Distance

**Read:** Isaiah 9:2; Isaiah 9:6

Hope begins with noticing a light we did not create.

Isaiah pictures people walking in darkness who encounter a great light. The passage does not begin with their success at finding a way out. It begins with a gift that reaches them where they are. The promise of a child places hope beside vulnerability rather than spectacle.

As December begins, you may feel pressure to manufacture a joyful mood. This passage offers another starting point: receive before you perform. You can acknowledge what is difficult and still make room for hope. Christian expectation looks toward God’s action, not toward our ability to make every circumstance bright. Let this month begin with attention to the light, however small your present glimpse of it may be.

**Put it into practice**

Name one place where you need hope. Sit quietly with that concern before making your plans for the day.

**Prayer**

God of light, meet me where I feel uncertain. Help me receive hope without pretending that darkness is not real. Teach me to notice your gifts. Amen.

## 2026-12-02 — Waiting Without Giving Up

**Read:** Psalms 130:5; Psalms 130:6

Waiting can be an expression of trust rather than an empty pause.

The psalmist waits for the LORD with the attentiveness of a watchman waiting for morning. A watchman cannot make the sun rise, but waiting is still purposeful work. The speaker anchors hope in God’s word rather than in a timetable he can control.

Some seasons ask us to live between a request and an answer. We may be tempted either to force an outcome or to stop caring. This psalm allows a third posture: remain attentive, keep praying, and do the faithful work available today. Waiting does not mean ignoring practical help or wise counsel. It means refusing to confuse the limits of our control with the limits of God’s care.

**Put it into practice**

Write down one unresolved situation and one constructive step you can take while you wait.

**Prayer**

Lord, steady my hope when answers are slow. Give me patience without passivity and courage without haste. Help me remain attentive to you today. Amen.

## 2026-12-03 — A Path Prepared

**Read:** Isaiah 40:3; Isaiah 40:4; Isaiah 40:5

Preparation makes room for the One we are expecting.

Isaiah uses the image of a road prepared through difficult country. Valleys, mountains, and rough ground become part of a vision in which the LORD’s glory is revealed. The language announces God’s coming rather than a project of self-improvement that earns his arrival.

Preparation can still shape our response. A crowded schedule, an unresolved quarrel, or a habit of constant distraction may keep us from paying attention. Making room need not be dramatic. One honest conversation or one quiet interval can become a way of turning toward God. The invitation is not to make yourself impressive. It is to become available, ready to receive and respond when grace interrupts your familiar routines.

**Put it into practice**

Remove one avoidable distraction from today and use the space for a short, unhurried prayer.

**Prayer**

Coming Lord, prepare my attention as well as my plans. Help me clear away what keeps me from listening and responding with an open heart. Amen.

## 2026-12-04 — Mercy That Meets Us

**Read:** Luke 1:76; Luke 1:77; Luke 1:78; Luke 1:79

The coming light is described as an expression of tender mercy.

In Zechariah’s song, the promised work of salvation is connected with forgiveness and God’s tender mercy. Light is pictured reaching those who sit in darkness and guiding their feet into peace. The movement is toward people in need, not away from them.

We sometimes imagine that closeness to God begins only after we have repaired everything about ourselves. This song points first to mercy. Forgiveness and guidance are gifts that meet people who need a way forward. Receiving mercy can also change how we regard others. Instead of reducing someone to a mistake or a difficult season, we can remember our own dependence on compassion. Peace grows where mercy becomes both something received and something practiced.

**Put it into practice**

Think of a person you have judged quickly. Replace one assumption with a sincere question or a prayer for their good.

**Prayer**

Merciful God, let your kindness soften my defensiveness. Guide my steps toward peace, and help me extend the patience I need from you. Amen.

## 2026-12-05 — Faithful in Ordinary Work

**Read:** Luke 1:8; Luke 1:9; Luke 1:11

An extraordinary announcement arrives during an ordinary responsibility.

Luke places the angel’s appearance to Zechariah within the work he was already doing. He is serving in the temple according to the duties assigned to him. The story’s surprising moment is not detached from daily faithfulness; it interrupts it.

Most days will not bring an astonishing announcement. Yet ordinary responsibilities are still places where attention, integrity, and care matter. Preparing food, answering a message, doing your work honestly, or keeping a commitment can be part of a faithful life. You do not need to abandon the ordinary in order to take God seriously. Begin with the task before you and the person affected by how you do it. Quiet faithfulness has value even when nobody notices.

**Put it into practice**

Choose one routine task and complete it with particular care for the person who will benefit.

**Prayer**

God of ordinary days, help me work with honesty and care. Keep me attentive to the people within my responsibilities and open to your unexpected invitations. Amen.

## 2026-12-06 — An Honest Question

**Read:** Luke 1:34; Luke 1:35; Luke 1:38

A question and a willing response can belong in the same conversation.

Mary asks how the angel’s announcement will happen. The response points to God’s action, and Mary expresses her willingness to receive the word spoken to her. Her question is part of the encounter rather than something the story hides.

Faith is sometimes described as though it leaves no room for uncertainty. But honest questions can help us listen more carefully. We can distinguish a desire to understand from a demand to control every outcome. Mary’s response does not provide a formula for making difficult choices easy. It offers a picture of willingness before everything is fully visible. Bring your questions into prayer, seek understanding, and consider what a faithful response might look like with the knowledge you presently have.

**Put it into practice**

Write one genuine question you have about faith. Bring it into prayer and identify a trustworthy person or source to explore it with.

**Prayer**

God, receive my questions without letting them become a wall against you. Give me understanding where it is needed and willingness for the next faithful step. Amen.

## 2026-12-07 — Encouragement on the Road

**Read:** Luke 1:39; Luke 1:40; Luke 1:45

A visit becomes a place where faith is recognized and encouraged.

After the announcement, Mary goes to visit Elizabeth. Their meeting is marked by welcome and blessing. Elizabeth’s words recognize Mary’s trust in what the Lord has spoken. A demanding calling is accompanied by the gift of another person’s encouragement.

We are not always able to remove someone else’s uncertainty, but we can help them feel less alone within it. A thoughtful visit, a listening ear, or a specific word of encouragement can strengthen a person facing change. Such care is different from promising that everything will turn out as we wish. It is the willingness to be present and to recognize what is good. Consider who has encouraged your faith, and who may need that gift from you.

**Put it into practice**

Send one specific message of encouragement that names something good you have noticed in another person.

**Prayer**

Lord, thank you for those who strengthen me through their presence. Make me attentive to someone who needs welcome, courage, or a listening ear today. Amen.

## 2026-12-08 — A Song Larger Than Myself

**Read:** Luke 1:46; Luke 1:47; Luke 1:52; Luke 1:53

Mary’s praise reaches beyond her own circumstances.

Mary begins by magnifying the Lord, then sings of a God who regards the lowly and fills the hungry. Her joy is personal, but its horizon is wider than private success. The song draws attention to people who are easily overlooked.

Prayer can become small when it contains only the things that would make our own lives more comfortable. Mary’s song invites us to enlarge our attention. Who is hungry, dismissed, or lacking power in the places we know? Praise need not separate us from those questions. It can turn us toward generosity and concern. Let gratitude for what you have received create a greater awareness of people whose needs might otherwise remain outside your daily view.

**Put it into practice**

Include a need beyond your immediate circle in prayer, then consider one practical way to respond.

**Prayer**

God who sees the lowly, enlarge my praise and my concern. Let gratitude move me toward generosity and keep me from overlooking another person’s need. Amen.

## 2026-12-09 — The Courage of Quiet Care

**Read:** Matthew 1:19; Matthew 1:20; Matthew 1:24

Joseph’s part in the story includes restraint, listening, and action.

Matthew describes Joseph considering how to respond to a painful and confusing situation. Before the dream’s instruction, he is already portrayed as unwilling to expose Mary to public shame. After receiving direction, he acts on it.

Not every faithful response is loud. Sometimes courage appears as restraint: refusing to humiliate someone, delaying a harsh conclusion, or listening before acting. Joseph’s story does not ask us to ignore the need for truth or wise boundaries. It reminds us that uncertainty is not permission to treat another person carelessly. When you do not yet know what to do, you can still choose not to add unnecessary harm. Patient attention can prepare the way for a clearer response.

**Put it into practice**

Pause before repeating a damaging assumption about someone. Seek clarity without making their situation public entertainment.

**Prayer**

Lord, help me respond to uncertainty with both truth and kindness. Teach me the courage of restraint and the readiness to act when the way becomes clear. Amen.

## 2026-12-10 — God with Us

**Read:** Matthew 1:22; Matthew 1:23

The name Emmanuel directs attention to God’s presence.

Matthew connects the birth announcement with the name Emmanuel, explained as God with us. The name speaks of presence rather than distance. The story asks readers to recognize God’s nearness in the coming of Jesus.

Presence does not mean that every difficulty immediately disappears. Being accompanied and being spared all pain are different things. When you feel alone, this name offers a focus for prayer: God is not merely a subject to think about, but the One to whom you can turn. It can also shape your care for others. We cannot solve every burden, yet we can refuse to leave people entirely alone with it. A faithful presence can be a meaningful gift.

**Put it into practice**

Offer someone an unhurried moment of attention, without feeling obliged to supply an immediate solution.

**Prayer**

Emmanuel, remind me of your nearness when I feel isolated. Help me offer patient presence to others and receive the care that reaches me through them. Amen.

## 2026-12-11 — A Journey with Uncertainty

**Read:** Luke 2:1; Luke 2:4; Luke 2:5

The birth narrative includes travel shaped by circumstances outside the family’s control.

Luke describes Joseph going from Nazareth to Bethlehem with Mary because of a registration. The journey places the family within larger political and social circumstances. The familiar story includes movement, obligation, and uncertainty, not only a peaceful final scene.

We often wish faith would give us complete control over the conditions of our lives. Yet faithful living usually happens within circumstances we did not choose. A changed plan, a required journey, or an inconvenient responsibility may become part of the setting in which we must respond. You do not need to call every disruption good. You can ask how to live with care and integrity within it, and where help is needed along the way.

**Put it into practice**

Identify one disrupted plan. Ask what is still within your responsibility and what support you can seek.

**Prayer**

God, travel with me through circumstances I cannot arrange. Give me steadiness for what I must do and humility to receive help on the journey. Amen.

## 2026-12-12 — Room for the Overlooked

**Read:** Luke 2:6; Luke 2:7

The story’s humble setting challenges our expectations of importance.

Luke describes Mary laying her firstborn son in a manger because there was no room for them in the inn. The detail places the birth in an unexpected setting. The child’s significance is not signaled by impressive surroundings.

We can learn to notice people by the status of the places they occupy: the best seat, the largest home, the most visible role. This passage unsettles that habit. Dignity is not created by recognition or comfort. As you prepare for Christmas, consider who is easy to miss in the activity around you. Making room may mean changing a plan, sharing a resource, or giving attention to someone whose circumstances are less convenient than your own.

**Put it into practice**

Notice one person at risk of being overlooked and make a specific, appropriate gesture of welcome.

**Prayer**

Jesus, born in humility, teach me to recognize dignity beyond appearances. Make room in my attention and my plans for people I might otherwise pass by. Amen.

## 2026-12-13 — Good News Beyond the Center

**Read:** Luke 2:8; Luke 2:9; Luke 2:10

The announcement reaches people keeping watch in the fields.

Luke’s shepherds are attending their flocks at night when the angel appears. Fear is met with a message of good news and great joy. The announcement is not confined to the most prominent people or places in the story.

You may sometimes feel that meaningful things happen elsewhere, among people with more influence, confidence, or opportunity. The shepherds’ encounter offers a different picture of attention. God’s message reaches them in the place where they are working. This does not make every ordinary event a hidden sign to decode. It invites openness: the familiar place is not beyond the reach of grace. Carry your responsibilities without assuming that your life is too small to matter.

**Put it into practice**

Pause during a routine part of your day and offer a short prayer of attentiveness.

**Prayer**

God of good news, meet me in the ordinary places of my life. Help me listen without assuming that your care is reserved for someone more important. Amen.

## 2026-12-14 — A Sign of Humility

**Read:** Luke 2:11; Luke 2:12

The sign given to the shepherds is a child lying in a manger.

The angel’s announcement names a Savior, Christ the Lord, and then describes a remarkably humble sign: a baby wrapped in cloths and lying in a manger. Great titles and a vulnerable child stand together in the same message.

We are used to associating power with the ability to impress or command attention. The Christmas story invites us to reconsider that connection. Humility is not the absence of significance. Care for the vulnerable is not a distraction from what matters most. As the season grows busy, look for the small responsibilities that can be lost beneath the search for a perfect occasion. A tender response may express faith more clearly than an impressive performance.

**Put it into practice**

Choose one small act of care that does not need an audience, and carry it out quietly.

**Prayer**

Christ the Lord, help me recognize the strength of humility. Turn my attention from appearances toward the real needs of those entrusted to my care. Amen.

## 2026-12-15 — Peace Worth Practicing

**Read:** Luke 2:13; Luke 2:14; Romans 12:18

Peace is celebrated in the birth announcement and practiced in our relationships.

Luke’s scene fills with praise to God and an announcement of peace. Reading this alongside Paul’s call to pursue peace reminds us that peace is more than a seasonal decoration. It concerns the way people meet one another.

Holiday gatherings can bring affection and tension into the same room. You cannot control every conversation or make every relationship safe by trying harder. You can attend to your own conduct: speak truthfully, avoid needless provocation, and honor appropriate boundaries. Peace is not pretending that harm never happened. Sometimes it begins with a measured answer, a willingness to listen, or a decision not to repeat an old pattern. Let praise become a practical posture.

**Put it into practice**

Prepare one peaceful response for a conversation you expect to find difficult.

**Prayer**

God of peace, guide my words and expectations. Help me pursue reconciliation where possible and keep wise boundaries where they are needed. Amen.

## 2026-12-16 — Go and See

**Read:** Luke 2:15; Luke 2:16

The shepherds respond to what they have heard by taking a step.

After the angels depart, the shepherds decide to go to Bethlehem. Luke describes them finding Mary, Joseph, and the baby. The announcement leads to movement; they do not remain only observers of an extraordinary moment.

There is a difference between being stirred by a message and responding to it. We can collect encouraging thoughts while postponing the ordinary actions they suggest. A response need not be grand or hurried. It may be the conversation, act of generosity, or request for help that you already know is needed. Consider what you have been hearing in Scripture this month. What would it mean to take one concrete step rather than wait for a stronger feeling?

**Put it into practice**

Choose one action prompted by your recent reading and decide when you will do it.

**Prayer**

Lord, help me move from hearing to responding. Give me a faithful next step and the willingness to take it without waiting to feel completely ready. Amen.

## 2026-12-17 — Treasuring What Matters

**Read:** Luke 2:19

Not every meaningful experience needs an immediate explanation.

Luke pauses the movement of the story to describe Mary keeping these things and pondering them in her heart. Announcements, visitors, and surprising events are met with reflection. The narrative leaves space for attention that is neither hurried speech nor instant certainty.

December can become a month of passing quickly from one obligation to another. Pondering asks something different of us: remember, consider, and allow meaning to unfold. You might reflect on a kindness, a difficult question, or a passage you do not yet understand. Quiet attention is not wasted time. It can help you distinguish what is merely urgent from what deserves to be carried with care into the days ahead.

**Put it into practice**

Set aside five quiet minutes. Write down one moment from this week that you want to remember and consider.

**Prayer**

God, give me space to ponder rather than rush past what matters. Help me treasure your gifts and bring my unanswered questions into your presence. Amen.

## 2026-12-18 — Return with Gratitude

**Read:** Luke 2:17; Luke 2:20

The shepherds return to ordinary life carrying a changed response.

The shepherds make known what they have heard and then return, glorifying and praising God. Their work does not disappear from the story. They go back, but their response to what they have seen accompanies them.

A meaningful time of prayer or worship eventually gives way to errands, work, and familiar responsibilities. The question is not how to preserve an unusual feeling forever. It is how gratitude can travel with us into ordinary life. You can return to a routine with greater patience, notice a person more carefully, or speak about a kindness you received. What we carry back may matter as much as what we experienced in the moment.

**Put it into practice**

Let one moment of gratitude shape how you approach a familiar responsibility today.

**Prayer**

Lord, let gratitude accompany me beyond quiet moments of worship. Help me return to ordinary duties with greater patience, attention, and joy. Amen.

## 2026-12-19 — Patient Expectation

**Read:** Luke 2:25; Luke 2:26; Luke 2:28; Luke 2:29

Simeon’s waiting is portrayed as attentive and hopeful.

Luke describes Simeon waiting for the consolation of Israel. When he encounters the child Jesus, he receives him with praise. His words place this meeting within a larger hope rather than treating it as a private achievement.

Long expectation can make us tired, especially when other people seem to move ahead more quickly. Simeon’s story invites attention to faithfulness across time. Waiting may include prayer, service, learning, and the support of a community. It need not become a test in which disappointment proves that we failed. Bring your hopes to God as they actually are. Allow others to accompany you, and stay open to goodness that arrives in an unexpected form.

**Put it into practice**

Name a hope you have carried for a long time. Share it with someone trustworthy or bring it into a written prayer.

**Prayer**

God of patient hope, receive what I have carried for many days. Keep disappointment from closing my heart, and help me recognize the good that comes. Amen.

## 2026-12-20 — Faithfulness That Makes Room

**Read:** Luke 2:36; Luke 2:37; Luke 2:38

Anna’s worship and gratitude become part of the welcome surrounding Jesus.

Luke introduces Anna as a prophetess whose life includes worship, fasting, and prayer. When she comes upon the scene, she gives thanks and speaks about the child to those awaiting redemption. A long life of devotion meets a particular moment of response.

Faithfulness is not always measured by novelty. Practices that seem repetitive can train our attention and make room for gratitude. Yet a habit is not a bargain that obliges God to reward us with a special experience. Prayer is a relationship to inhabit, not a result to manufacture. Consider one practice that helps you turn toward God. Keep it simple enough to sustain and open enough to lead you toward care for others.

**Put it into practice**

Choose a brief daily practice of prayer or thanksgiving that you can continue beyond this season.

**Prayer**

Lord, shape my attention through ordinary faithfulness. Let my habits make room for gratitude and keep them from becoming a measure of my superiority. Amen.

## 2026-12-21 — A Gift for the World

**Read:** John 3:16; John 3:17

God’s love is described with a horizon wider than our familiar circle.

John speaks of God loving the world and giving the Son. The following verse describes a saving purpose rather than a mission of condemnation. Love and giving belong together in the passage’s presentation of God’s action.

Gift-giving can easily become an exchange shaped by expectation, comparison, or obligation. This passage invites us to think first about love directed toward another’s good. We cannot reproduce God’s gift, but we can allow it to question the motives of our own generosity. Does a gift recognize the recipient as a person, or does it serve our wish to appear thoughtful? The most fitting gift may be modest, practical, and freely offered.

**Put it into practice**

Choose one gift of time, attention, or resources based on a genuine need rather than appearance.

**Prayer**

God of generous love, free my giving from comparison and performance. Help me receive your kindness and offer what I have with an open hand. Amen.

## 2026-12-22 — Light That Darkness Cannot Defeat

**Read:** John 1:4; John 1:5

John introduces life and light before describing the Word becoming flesh.

John’s opening presents life in the Word as the light of humanity and places that light against darkness. The image directs attention to a source of life beyond our own effort. Before describing the Word becoming flesh, John invites readers to consider this contrast between light and darkness.

Hope does not require us to deny what is wrong. We can grieve loss, oppose injustice, and acknowledge confusion while still turning toward the life revealed in Christ. Rather than demanding that you solve every darkness today, this passage invites a faithful orientation. Seek what gives life, tell the truth about what harms, and choose a response that does not multiply the darkness you are facing.

**Put it into practice**

Choose one small life-giving response to something discouraging: a truthful word, practical help, or patient presence.

**Prayer**

Living Word, shine into places I cannot make bright by myself. Guide me toward truth and care, and keep discouragement from becoming the whole story. Amen.

## 2026-12-23 — Preparing a Welcome

**Read:** Matthew 25:35; Matthew 25:36; Matthew 25:40

Welcoming Christ turns our attention toward tangible human need.

In Jesus’ picture of judgment, hunger, thirst, estrangement, inadequate clothing, sickness, and imprisonment receive particular attention. Service to those in need is treated with extraordinary seriousness. The passage draws faith toward practical care.

As Christmas approaches, preparation may center on the appearance of a room or the success of a gathering. Those things can express affection, but they are not the only forms of welcome. Who needs food, a call, a visit, or help finding support? You cannot meet every need alone. You can respond to one need with thoughtfulness and respect, perhaps by joining the work of people already serving well. Let preparation include a place for compassion.

**Put it into practice**

Identify a concrete need and respond directly or through a trustworthy local organization.

**Prayer**

Jesus, turn my preparation outward as well as inward. Help me recognize real needs and respond with generosity, humility, and respect for those receiving care. Amen.

## 2026-12-24 — The Nearness of Grace

**Read:** John 1:14

Christmas turns our attention to the Word dwelling among us.

John describes the Word becoming flesh and dwelling among us, full of grace and truth. The language centers on God’s nearness in embodied life. It does not present human vulnerability as something beneath divine concern.

On a day that may carry celebration, exhaustion, loneliness, or all three, remember that faith does not require a perfect setting. You can turn toward Christ in the actual conditions of your life. Grace and truth belong together: you do not need to decorate away what is painful, nor must pain erase every gift. Make a little room for prayer in the middle of what is real, and for kindness toward the people sharing that reality with you.

**Put it into practice**

Pause before the day ends. Name honestly how you are, and make one small gesture of care.

**Prayer**

Word made flesh, be near in the reality of this day. Meet celebration and loneliness alike with grace, and help me respond with truth and kindness. Amen.

## 2026-12-25 — Joy in a Humble Place

**Read:** Luke 2:10; Luke 2:11; Luke 2:12

The announcement of joy leads to a child, not an impressive display.

The angel announces good news of great joy and names the child as Savior, Christ the Lord. The sign is humble: a baby wrapped in cloths and lying in a manger. Christmas holds wonder and vulnerability together.

Your celebration may be full, quiet, complicated, or absent in the form you hoped for. The birth story does not ask you to measure its meaning by the quality of your day. Its center is a gift received, not a mood achieved. Allow gratitude to take a shape appropriate to your circumstances: a prayer, a shared meal, a moment of remembrance, or a gentle act of generosity. Joy can be received without demanding that every sorrow disappear.

**Put it into practice**

Read the birth announcement slowly and offer thanks in one way that is honest for your circumstances.

**Prayer**

Jesus Christ, thank you for coming in humility. Help me receive the gift of this day without comparison, and extend your kindness to someone near me. Amen.

## 2026-12-26 — Love After the Occasion

**Read:** Colossians 3:12; Colossians 3:13; Colossians 3:14

The work of love continues after a celebration ends.

Paul describes compassion, kindness, humility, gentleness, and patience as qualities to put on. He then speaks of bearing with one another, forgiveness, and love. These instructions concern the ordinary texture of shared life.

The day after a celebration may reveal tiredness, unfinished work, or disappointment. This is not a less important setting for faith. It may be precisely where kindness becomes concrete. A patient answer while cleaning up, a fair division of responsibilities, or acknowledgment of someone else’s effort can carry love beyond a special occasion. Forgiveness does not mean pretending that harm is harmless. It means seeking a response shaped by grace and truth, with boundaries and support where they are needed.

**Put it into practice**

Thank someone for an unnoticed contribution and take responsibility for one practical task.

**Prayer**

God of everyday love, help me practice compassion when I am tired. Make my gratitude practical and my patience more generous than my first reaction. Amen.

## 2026-12-27 — Remembering the Good

**Read:** Psalms 103:1; Psalms 103:2

Remembering can become an intentional act of praise.

The psalmist calls his own soul to bless the LORD and not forget God’s benefits. Praise is not presented only as a feeling that happens spontaneously. It includes the deliberate work of remembering.

Near the end of a year, the most difficult or recent events can dominate our account of what happened. Remembering goodness does not require editing grief out of the story. It means allowing the story to be fuller. Recall a person who helped, an opportunity to begin again, or a provision you did not know how to secure. Let those memories become gratitude rather than a demand that your life should always look the same. Remembering can strengthen attention for what comes next.

**Put it into practice**

Write down three specific gifts from this year, including at least one that arrived through another person.

**Prayer**

Faithful God, help me remember without denying loss. Receive my thanks for the good I have known, and keep my attention open to your continuing care. Amen.

## 2026-12-28 — Gentleness with Unfinished Work

**Read:** Philippians 1:6

The language of a continuing work leaves room for growth over time.

Paul expresses confidence that the one who began a good work will carry it toward completion. His words belong to a letter of affection and prayer for a community. The focus is God’s faithfulness, not a claim that every personal project will succeed.

At year’s end, unfinished goals may feel like a verdict on your worth. This passage invites a different measure. Growth in faith is not identical to an impressive list of achievements. Consider where you have become more truthful, patient, willing to learn, or ready to receive help. Also acknowledge what needs attention without turning it into self-contempt. A faithful next step can be small. You do not need to finish every chapter of your life before the calendar changes.

**Put it into practice**

Name one area of real growth and one next step that is modest enough to take.

**Prayer**

God who continues your work, keep me from pride and despair. Help me recognize growth honestly and meet unfinished places with patience and responsibility. Amen.

## 2026-12-29 — A Time to Listen

**Read:** James 1:19; James 1:20

Patient listening can change the way a difficult conversation unfolds.

James urges readiness to hear, slowness to speak, and slowness to anger. He connects this instruction with the limits of human anger in producing God’s righteousness. The passage asks for a disciplined response rather than an automatic reaction.

As a year closes, you may carry unresolved conversations. Not all can be repaired immediately, and not every conversation is safe to pursue. Where a conversation is appropriate, begin by listening to understand rather than collecting material for a reply. You can disagree without rushing to dismiss. A pause can make room for a question, a correction, or an apology. Patient attention is not weakness; it is a way of honoring the seriousness of both truth and another person.

**Put it into practice**

In one conversation, ask a clarifying question before offering your view.

**Prayer**

Lord, slow my impulse to react. Give me ears to listen, courage to speak truthfully, and humility to recognize what I have not yet understood. Amen.

## 2026-12-30 — Numbering Our Days

**Read:** Psalms 90:12; Psalms 90:17

Awareness of time can lead toward wisdom rather than panic.

Psalm 90 asks God to teach the numbering of days so that wisdom may grow. The prayer also asks that the work of human hands be established. Awareness of life’s limits leads into dependence and purposeful work.

A turning year can make time feel like an opponent. We may rush to recover what we think we wasted or promise more than we can sustain. Wisdom asks a quieter question: what deserves the time actually available to me? Some responsibilities are unavoidable, but attention can still be shaped. Consider what strengthens love, truthfulness, service, and rest. A limited life need not be a frantic one. Ask for help using your days with greater care rather than greater self-pressure.

**Put it into practice**

Choose one commitment to protect and one avoidable demand to release as you prepare for the new year.

**Prayer**

God of our days, teach me wisdom about my limits. Establish what is good in my work, and help me make room for what matters most. Amen.

## 2026-12-31 — Entrusting the Year

**Read:** Psalms 31:14; Psalms 31:15

The year can be entrusted to God without a tidy ending.

The psalmist expresses trust and places his times in God’s hand while still asking for deliverance. Confidence and need are present together. The prayer does not wait until every threat has passed before turning toward God.

You may finish this year with gratitude, regret, relief, or unanswered questions. There is no requirement to force these into a simple conclusion. Bring the whole year into prayer: what you received, what you lost, what you did well, and what needs confession or repair. Entrusting the past is not refusing responsibility. It is acknowledging that you cannot relive it, while remaining willing to respond faithfully to what it has left in your care.

**Put it into practice**

Write a short year-end prayer with three parts: thank you, forgive me, and help me.

**Prayer**

Lord, my times are in your hand. Receive this year with its gifts and wounds. Give me courage for repair, gratitude for grace, and trust for what lies ahead. Amen.
